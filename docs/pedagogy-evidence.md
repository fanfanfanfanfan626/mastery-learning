# Pedagogy evidence and claim boundary

Mastery Learning uses research-informed design rules. That phrase is deliberate: evidence for a teaching principle does not by itself prove that this particular Codex plugin improves learner outcomes. Product effectiveness requires direct evaluation of the installed plugin with real conversations and, separately, consenting learners.

## Evidence map

| Product rule | Research basis | Design implication | Important boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieve before rereading | Roediger and Karpicke found better delayed retention after retrieval practice than repeated study in experiments with prose passages. | Start sessions with recall or application and do not equate fluent rereading with durable knowledge. | The study does not establish transfer to programming, mathematics, or long projects. |
| Space consequential review | Cepeda and colleagues synthesized 317 experiments and found that spacing and the desired retention interval interact. Dunlosky and colleagues rated distributed practice and practice testing as high-utility techniques. | Keep review obligations separate from ordinary practice; do not let early or assisted work postpone due retrieval. | The plugin's fixed early intervals are conservative defaults, not an individually validated scheduling model. |
| Require criteria plus corrective work | Mastery-learning programs in Kulik, Kulik, and Bangert-Drowns's meta-analysis generally improved examination performance and attitudes. | Keep concepts provisional until required evidence exists; allow correction and reassessment rather than advancing on exposure alone. | Effects varied by procedure, design, and content; self-paced programs could reduce completion rates and increase time on task. |
| Give causal, actionable feedback | Hattie and Timperley reviewed feedback as a major influence whose effect can be positive or negative depending on type and delivery. | Point to the earliest causal gap, give one useful correction or hint, and request a retry. | More feedback is not automatically better; praise, answer revelation, and task-level correction have different effects. |
| Test changed-context use | Barnett and Ceci showed that transfer varies along multiple dimensions rather than being a single yes/no property. | Require a changed-context transfer or project event in addition to recall and immediate exercise success. | Passing one transfer task does not establish unrestricted far transfer. |
| Personalize without fixed learning-style labels | Pashler and colleagues found inadequate evidence for matching instruction to assessed learning styles. | Adapt sequence, examples, representation, and hint size as revisable hypotheses based on observed performance. | Learner preferences still matter for access, motivation, and usability; they are not proof of a fixed cognitive type. |

## Product-specific hypotheses

The following remain hypotheses until evaluated directly:

1. Evidence gates reduce false mastery declarations compared with an unstructured Codex learning conversation.
2. Delayed retrieval plus changed-context tasks improve later independent performance.
3. Local structured handoffs improve cross-task continuity without encouraging transcript surveillance.
4. Tool-generation gates improve practice quality without leaking assessed answers.
5. A complete coverage graph plus a short active path reduces omission without increasing overload.

Program tests can show that these rules are encoded and that state transitions are correct. Conversation evaluations can show whether Codex follows the rules consistently. Only learner-outcome studies can estimate educational benefit.

## Allowed and disallowed claims

Acceptable before outcome studies:

- “Research-informed mastery-learning workflow.”
- “Requires independent, delayed, and transfer evidence before recording mastery.”
- “The state and tool contracts pass the published automated tests.”
- “Conversation behavior was evaluated on the published synthetic suite,” only when the corresponding result and transcript evidence are published.

Not acceptable without direct learner evidence:

- “Proven to improve learning.”
- “Learners retain knowledge significantly longer.”
- “Scientifically optimized review intervals.”
- “More effective than teachers, courses, tutors, or ordinary Codex.”
